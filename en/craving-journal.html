Alcohol Cravings: Spot Your Triggers and Consequences in a Journal

A drinking diary shows you how much you drink. It doesn't show you why. For that you need a second journal, one with no grams in it, only situations, feelings and consequences: What was going on when the craving came? What did you do? How did you feel the next morning? This is where the real work happens, because only once you know your drinking triggers can you plan something else for them.

Why a separate journal for the reasons?

Mixing numbers and reasons sounds practical but rarely works. The numbers take a few seconds. The reasons need a few quiet minutes and honest sentences. Squeeze both into one table and the "mood" column soon says nothing but "fine".

The second journal has three jobs:

  1. Spot your triggers. In which situations, and with which feelings, does the wish for a drink come up?
  2. Record the consequences. How did the evening really go, and how did the morning after feel?
  3. Plan alternatives. What could you do instead next time, and did it work?

Three moments to write

When the craving hits

This is the most important entry, and it counts even if you end up not drinking. Counsellors call it a craving or an urge to drink: simply the strong wish to have a drink right now. Write down:

  • Where are you, and who's with you?
  • What happened just before?
  • What's the feeling: stress, boredom, anger, loneliness, relief, the urge to celebrate?
  • How strong is the craving on a scale from 1 to 10?
  • What thought comes with it? Often it's something like "I've earned this" or "One glass won't hurt".
  • What did you do then, and how strong was the craving a little later?

Writing it down already buys you time. A craving builds, peaks and fades again, often sooner than you expect. If you spend that moment writing, you no longer decide at the peak.

On a day you drank

This isn't about the amount; that goes in the drinking diary. It's about the path to the first glass:

  • What was the trigger?
  • How much did you plan to drink, and how much did it turn into?
  • When did you notice it was going to be more than planned?
  • What did you expect the drink to do for you, and did it?

The morning after

After an evening that went further than you wanted, write down the consequences while you still feel them:

  • How did you sleep?
  • How do you feel, physically and in your head?
  • What did the evening cost?
  • Is there something you said or did that you'd rather not have?
  • What's on today that's now harder to do?

This isn't a punishment. These entries are for the next evening when the craving tells you how good it was last time. Then you can read how it really was.

From triggers to alternatives

After a while, your entries show which situations keep coming back. Often it isn't a long list of alcohol triggers, just the same few moments repeating: getting home after a hard day, meeting certain people, an evening alone in front of the TV, an argument.

For each of these situations, decide now what you'll do instead, and write it as an if-then sentence. Psychologists call these if-then plans. Studies show that concrete plans like these are carried out far more often than general intentions like "I want to drink less", because the decision has already been made and doesn't have to be made in the moment.

If … then …
I get home after a stressful day I go for a walk round the block and shower before I have anything
I'm out with colleagues on Friday my first drink is one without alcohol
I'm bored alone in the evening I call someone or do something with my hands
I've had an argument I write down what's bothering me first and decide after that
someone tops up my glass without asking I put my hand over it and say I'm taking a break

Your sentences don't have to look like these. What matters is that they fit your life and that the "then" is something you can do straight away. Over time you'll see which plans work and which don't, and adjust the ones that don't.

In Relimie both journals sit together: you log your drinks, and every day has a journal entry. The journal asks a question that fits the day. Frequently asked questions

Do I have to write every day?
No. Write on the days when something happened: when a craving came, when you drank, and the morning after an evening that didn't go as planned. On quiet days, one sentence or nothing at all is fine.

What if I gave in to the craving?
Write it down anyway, especially then. One entry about an evening that didn't work out tells you more about your triggers than plenty of entries about evenings that went well.

Does this help if I only want to drink less, not quit?
Yes. Writing down triggers and consequences helps with both goals. When you're cutting down, it's usually about finding the evenings that regularly turn into more than planned.

I can't think of an alternative. What now?
Start small. An alternative doesn't have to save the evening, it only has to get you past the first wave: a glass of water, a short walk outside, a phone call. Often the craving is weaker afterwards.

Does a journal like this replace professional help?
No. If cravings come very often, are very strong, or you notice you can't manage them on your own, talk to your doctor or an alcohol support service. Take the journal with you; it helps in that conversation too.
