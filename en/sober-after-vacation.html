Sober After Vacation: The Challenge You Were Missing

You came home with a tan, three thousand photos, a fridge magnet nobody needed, and a week of drinking that looked nothing like your normal week. That is what vacations are. Nobody books an all-inclusive trip planning to be moderate, and there is no version of this article that tells you off for it.

But there is a moment, usually somewhere between unpacking the suitcase and the first Monday back, when a quiet question shows up: how far off track am I, really? Most people never answer it. They either shrug it away or carry a vague bad feeling around for a week or two, which helps nobody. The useful answer is a number, and it is easier to get than you would think.

The welcome drink you didn't order

Vacation drinking has its own rituals, and you never consciously decide on any of them at the time.

  • The welcome drink. It arrives before your room does. You didn't ask for it. You drink it.
  • Beer o'clock is a time zone. At home you would never open something at four in the afternoon. On a lounger, four in the afternoon is practically the evening.
  • The buffet wine loop. The glass is small, the walk back is short, and somehow the bottle is a memory.
  • Minibar math. Two of those tiny bottles are one normal drink, which feels like a discount rather than an equation.
  • "It's included." Not a reason. Still a fact.

None of this needs defending. It is the whole point of going away. It just means the week you came back from is not a good sample of who you are the rest of the year, and it does not vanish on its own because the tan is fading.

What a week away adds up to

Alcohol is counted in grams of pure alcohol, because a small strong drink and a large weak one can carry exactly the same amount. Here is what a fairly ordinary all-inclusive day looks like when you write it down.

Drink Serving Pure alcohol (approx.)
Cocktails by the pool 2 x 50 ml spirits (40%) ~32 g
Wine with dinner 2 x 200 ml (12%) ~38 g
Big beers on the terrace 2 x 500 ml (5%) ~40 g
Day total ~110 g

Nothing there looks like a bender. It is three moments spread over twelve hours, each of which felt reasonable while it was happening, and the pours are the ones you get handed on vacation rather than the tidy measures on a chart. Over seven nights it comes to roughly 770 grams of pure alcohol, which is also a fair number of calories.

Why the pause comes after, not during

An average only means anything over time. One loud week does not define your year, so there is no sense in feeling bad about it. It also does not cancel itself out. It sits in your average until quieter days balance it.

People tend to tell themselves one of two stories after a trip: it was only a week away so it doesn't count, or they have completely ruined everything. Arithmetic sits between the two, and it is much easier to live with than either. If you want the wider context on what typical drinking looks like, we've written about that separately.

Sober days to baseline

Relimie works from a personal baseline, a long-term daily average you are aiming to stay around rather than a hard limit you must never cross. You set it when you start, and you can change it whenever your life changes. There is more on choosing one in the user guide.

Once a baseline exists, the post-vacation question becomes a simple sum. Say your baseline is 20 g a day and you averaged around 110 g across seven nights away:

  • Over that week you drank about 770 g.
  • Your baseline across the same seven days would have been 140 g.
  • So you are carrying roughly 630 g above your own line.
  • Each sober day banks the 20 g you didn't use, so it takes about 32 sober days to bring the average back.

A week away, a month to level out. The vague unease becomes a finish line with a date on it.

Relimie does this sum for you and keeps it current as you log: tap the Orb on the home screen and it flips to show where you stand and how many sober days bring your average back in line. Change your baseline and the number moves. Log a drink and the number moves. It is your own arithmetic on your own inputs, which is also its limit: it is not a health timeline and not a verdict, and Relimie is an alcohol tracker, not medical advice.

Log it roughly, not perfectly

I didn't track any of it, so it's too late.

It isn't. Approximate is enough. You are not filing taxes, you are estimating an average, and an average survives a fair amount of rounding. Think in typical days rather than exact ones: about two glasses of wine most evenings, a beer most afternoons, a couple of bigger nights. That will land close enough for the number to be useful.

In Relimie you can go back to any past date and fill it in. Open the Consumption History, tap through to the day, and log it. Days you didn't drink get marked too: tap the Orb on an empty past date and it records a clear day. The FAQ walks through logging past days step by step.

Twenty minutes with your photo roll as a memory aid and the trip is in. The number is there as soon as you finish.

Make it your challenge

A number works better with an end date attached, and there are three sensible ways to set one.

Run your exact number. Whatever it says, nine days or twenty-two or thirty-one, that is your finish line. It is the most motivating version because nobody else's number looks like it.

Round it to something memorable. Thirty days is a satisfying shape and easy to explain to anyone who asks why you're on the sparkling water. If your number is close to it, round up and take the credit.

Ride the calendar. A whole calendar month is the tidiest container you will get: a start date you cannot forget and an end date you can count to. October already has a name for this one, sober October, and a summer trip tends to produce a number in about that range anyway. Come back in August or September and the month is already sized for you.

Whichever you pick, watch it in your analytics as the days accumulate. The line you are waiting for is the one where your average crosses back under your baseline.

Next time, pack it

The only annoying part of all this is the backfilling, and it is avoidable.

Vacations are full of dead minutes that are exactly long enough to log a drink: while the towel dries, the sunscreen minute, the boarding queue, waiting for someone to find your reservation. Two taps a day, and you land at home with the number already waiting for you.

That is the version to aim for next summer. This summer, rough is fine.

Frequently asked questions

How accurate does my vacation logging have to be?
Rough is genuinely fine. You are working out an average across a couple of weeks, and averages tolerate estimates well. Think in typical days rather than exact ones and log those. A roughly logged trip beats a perfectly forgotten one every time.

Is the days-to-baseline number a health timeline?
No. It is arithmetic on the amounts you entered, your logged intake measured against the baseline you set yourself. It tells you nothing about your body and it is not medical advice. Relimie is an alcohol tracker, nothing more.

What if my number comes out longer than a month?
Then it is a longer challenge. You can also revisit your baseline: if the one you set is stricter than the life you lead, adjust it. The baseline is meant to move as your life does.

Do I have to stop drinking completely to bring my average down?
No. Sober days bring it back fastest, which is why they make the cleanest challenge, but any day below your baseline moves the number in the right direction.

Do I need an account to do this?
No. Relimie works offline on your device, with no account and no sign-up. Your trip, your numbers and your diary stay with you.
